# Can a starch-source effect predict a differently prepared food?

**The effect did not transfer as a fixed property of the grain.** A cooking-specific model learned from three isolated starches predicted far too large an insulin reduction for baked high-amylose wheat. Separately, a milling reference that distinguished barley from oats predicted the opposite ordering of their flake-thickness effects. In the wheat trial, a much simpler food measurement came closer. These results narrow the claim INSIGHT can make about source and preparation. Decision D remains open.

## A cooking-specific amylose model fails on bread

In the Bornet development data, 18 people were assigned to three groups. We used six reported means: the three botanical sources in raw suspensions and the same three in cooked-and-cooled gels. Within each state, we fit log three-hour insulin area against log amylopectin fraction. Three sources gave a slope of −3.600 raw and +1.728 heated. A common slope fit after centering within preparation was −0.936. The development result captures part of the previously reported cassava/wheat reversal. Botanical source and amylose vary together. Dropping just one source gives heated slopes from 1.272 to 2.250; even the sign of the raw slope depends on retaining cassava. [Training data](training.json) · [Fitted models](model_results.json) · [Bornet original](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/50.2.315)

The frozen heated forecast for a new bread is proportional to its serving of starch times its amylopectin fraction raised to 1.728. Its ratios are consistent across choice of reference. Linear scaling by served starch is an additional dose assumption because Bornet supplied 35 g in every condition. Belobrajdic et al. tested a four-cell wheat-variety-by-flour-milling design. Nineteen adults completed the crossover. Each serving was 121 g of refined or wholemeal bread made with conventional (24% amylose) or high-amylose (74.3%) wheat. The breads differed in starch grams, protein, moisture and fibre as well as starch composition. All were baked, cooled, frozen, thawed and served without crust. The high-amylose bread is well beyond the training range of 17–35%. [Wheat trial, Tables 1–3 and Figure 2B](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/nxz067)

| High / conventional wheat | Refined bread | Wholemeal bread |
|---|---:|---:|
| Frozen heated model, including served starch | 0.124 | 0.114 |
| Amylopectin grams alone | 0.274 | 0.250 |
| Reported starch grams | 0.810 | 0.740 |
| Reported starch less resistant starch | 0.684 | 0.628 |
| Observed signed two-hour mean-curve ratio | 0.606 | 0.640 |

The models above used no insulin from the evaluation study. Its Table 3 reports **57.8 ± 6.1 versus 87.8 ± 9.3** (mean ± SEM, published three-hour high/conventional marginal areas). Their ratio is **0.658**, a 34.2% reduction; the prose reports 24%. The four individual three-hour cells are unavailable from this table. The model predicted 0.120 for the high/conventional marginal; the reported starch ratio was 0.779, the paper-defined carbohydrate ratio 0.790, and starch less resistant starch **0.660**. Across positive flour weights shared by numerator and denominator, the heated model's marginal must lie between its two stratum forecasts, **0.114–0.124**. Changing a common milling multiplier cannot repair the miss. The 2019 table describes carbohydrate as starch plus sugar. Subtraction of separately measured resistant starch is a sensitivity calculation; the close ratio does not establish a general law. [Four bread comparisons and separate margin results](wheat_results.json)

**The failure extends beyond signed mean curves under an explicit sampling assumption.** For a person with nonnegative concentration C and baseline B, the positive excursion is bounded below by C − B and above by C. Integrating, then averaging, bounds the mean individual positive area between the signed and gross mean-curve areas. A conservative outer ratio interval on the displayed 0/15/30/60/120-minute grid is **0.382–1.098** for refined bread and **0.378–1.255** for wholemeal, allowing for coordinate-reading uncertainty. Both the 1.728 slope and plain amylopectin dose fall below both lower bounds. Sol's independent coordinates give lower bounds of 0.374 and 0.380. All three leave-one-source-out heated forecasts also fall short. The bounds assume a fixed included sample within each arm and piecewise-linear trajectories on the displayed grid. They are neither observed participant areas nor population intervals. The methods mention additional blood draws at 45 and 90 minutes, but Figure 2B shows no insulin values there. Four high IAUC responses, one bread per affected participant, were excluded from the authors' IAUC analysis. We did not silently align that analysis with the graph. Table and graph absolute numerical scales also differ; no ad hoc unit conversion was applied. [Bound calculation](positive_results.json) · [Model restrictions](extrapolation_results.json)

For a linear serving-dose score proportional to starch grams times amylopectin fraction raised to an exponent, the same outer lower bounds require an exponent no greater than about **0.693 refined** and **0.619 wholemeal**. These are necessary conditions on that model in this sample, not replacement coefficients. A two-pool score that assigns amylopectin weight one and amylose an unknown weight would need that second weight to be at least 0.161 and 0.212, respectively, under the same assumptions. This calculation does not identify biological insulin potencies. [Restriction calculation](extrapolation_results.json)

## Opus tested whether amylose adds information after accounting for starch dose

Opus fitted one partial-contribution coefficient using **only two previously exposed covered-barley high/normal contrasts**, one for kernels and one for flour. The reported barley ratios were 0.897 and 0.927. The joint coefficient was **0.259** (kernels only 0.167, flour only 0.362). The score multiplies the starch dose by `(1 − A) + θA`, where A is the amylose fraction of total starch. It was tested unchanged on the wheat breads, where amylose spanned 24–74.3% versus 33–42% in training. These studies do not establish equivalence of the barley potentially available starch method and wheat starch less RS. On the subtracted-dose basis, multiplying by a term based on total-starch composition can count the same accessibility difference twice. The coefficient has an empirical interpretation only.

| Wheat forecast | Refined high / normal | Wholemeal high / normal | Two-contrast mean absolute log error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Starch less resistant starch | 0.684 | 0.628 | **0.070** |
| Joint barley coefficient × starch less RS | 0.374 | 0.343 | **0.553** |
| Joint barley coefficient × total starch | 0.443 | 0.405 | **0.386** |
| Total starch | 0.810 | 0.740 | **0.217** |
| Equal response | 1.000 | 1.000 | **0.474** |

The barley coefficient reduced the much larger error of the isolated-gel exponent, but adding it made **both** starch-dose baselines worse on this endpoint. The flour-only barley coefficient on the subtracted dose did beat equality (0.425 vs 0.474), while still losing to the unadjusted subtracted dose. A coefficient cannot distinguish wholemeal and refined arms of the same wheat variety when their assigned amylose fractions coincide. The checked memo and executable retain these successes alongside the failures. The test does not establish a biological weight for amylose or require removing it from every model. [Barley-trained comparison](amylose_transfer_results.json) · [Independent checks](sol/opus/transfer_v2_verification.json)

At the joint coefficient, the total-starch forecasts 0.443 and 0.405 fall within the conservative positive-area intervals, even though they undershoot the signed point estimates. On the subtracted basis the refined forecast 0.374 is at the boundary: below the coordinator’s lower bound and just above Sol’s. The wholemeal forecast 0.343 falls below both sets of lower bounds. The native-positive rejection of the Bornet slope does not extend to every barley-trained alternative. [Envelope comparison](transfer_bounds_results.json)

## Can the simpler starch comparison travel?

Costabile et al. served 20 adults a conventional breakfast or one with 70% or 85% high-amylose flour in the bread formulation. These percentages describe flour substitution, not the amylose content of the meal. The breakfasts were matched at 80 g available carbohydrate, including 60 g from bread. The served portions contained 1, 7 and 8 g resistant starch. First-breakfast four-hour insulin iAUC was **13,200**, **14,000** and **13,500** for control, 70% and 85%, respectively. Both high-amylose/control ratios were near equality (**1.061** and **1.023**). Signed two-hour curves gave 0.957 and 0.938. The published four-hour areas and the graph integrals differ in absolute size. The paper's prose gives glucose units for insulin area; the figure labels insulin in µU/mL. The authors measured starch but did not publish total starch grams for these servings. Equal available carbohydrate has coverage here; the exact starch-minus-RS forecast remains unavailable. The larger later-meal effect belongs to a different challenge. In Hoebler’s separately measured breads, total starch minus RS predicted 0.791 for high-amylose versus standard bread, against a signed two-hour ratio of 0.578. Its spaghetti/standard prediction was 0.965 against 0.596. The favorable 2019 result does not transfer in magnitude. [Submitted starch comparisons](rs_transfer_results.json) [Costabile original](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2022.10.007) · [First-meal results](support/breakfast/comparisons.csv)

A further within-trial check compared refined high-amylose bread with conventional wholemeal bread. The authors report 40 g carbohydrate for both servings. Their signed two-hour area ratio was 0.707. Table carbohydrate predicted 1.018 and starch less RS predicted 0.895. This outcome-exposed contrast changes source and milling together; it keeps the favorable 0.660 marginal ratio in perspective. [Equal-carbohydrate comparison](equal_carbohydrate_results.json)

## A grain's milling effect does not specify its rolling effect

Granfeldt's flake study measured all seven flake breakfasts and a reference bread in ten volunteers. It provides matched 1.0-mm versus 0.5-mm comparisons within roasted oats and within steamed barley at 50 g starch. The original cohort and earlier barley trial share investigators and site; overlap of individual participants is undocumented. The oat and barley pretreatments differ, so the cross-grain comparison combines source with that processing context. [Original methods and Tables 1, 2, 4 and 6](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/130.9.2207)

| Thick / thin | External whole-kernel/flour reference | Observed two-hour area ratio |
|---|---:|---:|
| Roasted oats | 0.840 | 10.8 / 14.9 = 0.725 |
| Steamed barley | 0.519 (two varieties, geometric mean) | 15.3 / 14.7 = 1.041 |

The predicted oat/barley ratio-of-ratios was **1.620** versus **0.696** observed. Any common positive exponent applied to these milling ratios preserves their ordering; reducing the exponent toward zero cannot reverse it. Retaining either barley donor, allowing for its missing tenth flour participant and allowing for printed rounding preserves the directional conflict. A source-independent thickness rule predicts 1 for the double ratio and is closer on that objective. For oats alone, its own 0.840 reference is closer than either equality or the common grain reference. Thickness-specific gelatinization measurements were unavailable. Table 2 supplies a single row for the relevant pretreatment, not two independently assayed thicknesses. There was no significant paired insulin thickness difference established in either grain, and no reported test of the cross-grain interaction. These are sample forecasts. ## Does eliminating reference dependence improve prediction?

In the reviewed Bornet analysis, the oriented affine rule gives **4.195** for raw pea/cassava directly and **1.461** by dividing the two wheat-referenced forecasts. Its old oriented scores stay intact. For the new test we defined a score for each food: **14.276 + d**, where d is the measured grams of early starch release in the serving. The offset is fixed by one independent hummus contrast and expressed in its assay's gram-equivalent units. Dividing scores gives the same ratio through every reference. This formula predicts relative responses only; a portion-independent offset is an extrapolation, not evidence of an insulin response at zero food intake.

In eight adults studied by Hoebler et al., the observed signed two-hour high-amylose bread/white bread and spaghetti/white bread ratios were **0.578** and **0.596**. The coherent score predicted **0.821** and **0.721** using H30; direct released grams predicted **0.761** and **0.627**. Mean absolute log error on those two ratios was 0.271 for the coherent score, 0.404 for the oriented affine rule, 0.163 for direct release and 0.533 for equality. On the separate high-amylose bread/spaghetti contrast the observed ratio was 0.969. The coherent score predicted 1.139 and direct release predicted 1.214. Total starch alone predicted 89/91 = 0.978. That favorable central comparison has no paired-error interval. The early assay used ground freeze-dried breads but cut freshly cooked pasta, and the donor was read at 20 minutes rather than 30. The conversion from reported glucose-equivalent hydrolysis to starch grams is conditional; the offset's answer changes with that scale. The original Table 1 also reports 91 g total and 95 g available starch for spaghetti. The normalized insulin indices of 57 and 61 relative to white bread are kept separate from native areas. [Hoebler et al., Tables 1–2 and Figures 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1600718) · [All three comparisons](assay_results.json)

There is a structural limit on that last candidate. For any common nonnegative offset, the score ratio lies between 1 and the direct-release ratio. Across the declared ±2-pixel reading sensitivities, its lowest possible predictions were 0.746 for high-amylose bread/white bread and 0.613 for spaghetti/white bread. Both exceed the respective signed clinical upper readings, 0.591 and 0.609. The second gap is small. Increasing the offset can only move those predictions toward equality. This post-result restriction concerns these plotted means; it is not an assay-error distribution or population interval. [Offset-family check](offset_bound_results.json)

The barley-trained residual cannot be scored on Hoebler’s pasta/bread pair from the published composition used here. Bread wheat and durum wheat do not establish a common amylose fraction. Assuming that fraction equal would reduce the model to its starch-dose rule. The matched-carbohydrate breakfast also lacks the serving data needed to score the residual. Missing predictions are excluded from the error comparison.

## Decision

Keep source, actual process, served carbohydrate and measured release as distinct research inputs. Neither a grain-specific milling coefficient nor a slope learned from cooked isolated starch is portable across these food transformations. The measured carbohydrate comparison and the direct-release exceptions remain useful. A defensible next test would vary digestible starch independently of resistant starch within a specified recipe, with reported serving-level amounts and a first-meal insulin outcome. It would distinguish the promising 2019 measurement from the cheaper available-carbohydrate comparator. No absolute food-input insulin equation or consumer score was established.
