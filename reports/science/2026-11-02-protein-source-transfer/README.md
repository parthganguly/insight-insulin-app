# Protein source, dose and mixture prediction

A fixed adjustment for the name of the protein failed to improve prediction across held-out first authors. That failure coexists with two useful results. A measured response to each of two component drinks predicted the response to their combination closely in one controlled crossover. A food-input leucine proxy anticipated two replacement contrasts on average, even though total protein predicted the six two-hour arm levels more accurately.

## Across publications

We extracted all 78 rows of the insulin-area forest plot for participants without diabetes and used all 57 labelled 120 minutes. Before opening the full rows we fixed the predictors, grouping, missing-label rule and loss. Some historical study results, the overall estimate and the start of the forest plot were already exposed. This is retrospective model development with held-author evaluation. For each prediction all rows with the same first author were withheld. Each training author and each evaluation author had total weight one. The constant predicts the training geometric mean response ratio. The dose model fits log ratio on added protein per gram of carbohydrate with an intercept. The last candidate adds a source-specific residual averaged over other authors with one zero-residual pseudo-author. Its coverage defaults to the dose-only prediction for a previously unseen label. The primary criterion failed. On the reported panel the source term improved five author averages and worsened six. It changed 23 of 26 predictions. Restricting to these changed rows, error rose from 0.1325 to 0.1407. Dropping one author's **evaluation errors** without refitting gave a change in average error ranging from −0.0037 to +0.0149. In the calculated panel one author improved, one tied and seven worsened. Both panels favor a broad forecast of an increase over predicting no change. Neither establishes a population advantage for one level of model complexity. [Predictions and author-level errors](transfer/RESULTS.json)

The plot reports ratios of mean areas. Its 120-minute tag refers to the area window. Accepted reported areas need not share a below-fasting convention. The reviewers could also integrate the digitized group-mean curve and assumed an SD of 25% for those calculated areas. They used a within-person correlation assumption for crossover intervals. Those intervals do not quantify prediction-loss uncertainty. Holding out the first author's name cannot exclude all shared laboratories, protocols or participants. The two panels remain separate. Gunnerud 2013 appears both in this plot and in the direct whey test below, so its evidence is counted once. The supplement lists 12 participants. The original reported analysis used 11 after an insulin-response-based exclusion. ## Can the broader model explain the high-dose whey miss?

We applied the same reported-area estimator to the previously exposed Thondre 2026 drinks, excluding the two older Thondre rows from training. This is a post-result application. It predicts the whey/pea ratio as 1.175 at both powder doses. The printed ratios are 1.028 and 1.472. A fixed source offset cancels in their ratio of ratios. The common dose slope and slight differences in protein grams predict 1.00016 against 1.43218 observed. The source-aware four-arm error was 0.1127, versus 0.1069 for dose and 0.1036 for the constant. The earlier leucine gain on the higher pea arm and the fixed-whey rescaling bound still stand. [Application](transfer/ANCHOR_RESULTS.json) · [Thondre et al.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0340386)

There is a concrete limit to learning a better slope from these inputs. After that author exclusion, the only pea observations in the reported panel are three different split-pea-flour breads, **all at 0.06 g/g**. Only Gunnerud provides multiple whey doses within an author. A pea-isolate dose curve learned from this panel would therefore require extra assumptions about how bread and drinks compare. [Support inventory](transfer/SUPPORT.json) · [Fahmi et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods11070998)

A separate, pre-scoring transfer used Thondre's exposed actual protein grams to develop one slope each for whey alone, pea alone and both together. Tested on Gunnerud 2013's 4.5, 9 and 18 g whey drinks, whey-only beat no change at every dose. Average error was 0.110, compared with 0.316 for no change, 0.109 for the Lan curve and 0.083 for the pooled pea-and-whey slope. Using protein per gram of glucose doubled the effective coordinates because Gunnerud served 25 g glucose to Thondre's 50 g. Whey's error rose to 0.3165. The secondary Lan plateau at an effective 36 g is an explicit extension beyond its original 30 g domain. Only the 18 g clinical contrast was distinguished from glucose by the original adjusted comparison. No target insulin measurement enters the food-input ratio forecasts. ## Replacing intact protein with a specified amino-acid mixture

Opus implemented a test in a 14-person crossover with a ham sandwich and a pre-meal drink. Replacing 4.5 of 9 g intact whey or soy with five specified free amino acids preserves total added grams but increases leucine. For the source's two-hour insulin index, the average error across six arm levels was 0.044 for total protein, 0.089 for the leucine proxy and 0.166 for no change. On the two half-replacement **contrasts**, leucine did better on average than predicting a tie, 0.0359 vs 0.0833. Soy drove that advantage. The whey contrast had virtually identical errors and its error ranking can reverse within printed precision. At 30 minutes leucine had the lowest six-arm level error (0.099). It also improved both replacement point contrasts. All two-hour clinical indices shared a Tukey group. Neither replacement pair was distinguished at 30 minutes. Amino-acid profile and form vary together. [Gunnerud et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0044731) · [Opus's contrast correction](public/opus-experiment/RECONCILIATION_v2.md) · [Subsequent qualifications](public/opus-experiment/ADDENDUM.md)

The reported two-hour outcome is a sandwich-referenced insulin index. The early comparison uses ratios of group mean 30-minute areas. Neither supplies a food-only forecast of native absolute two-hour area. Tables 3 and 4 give 12–14 participants per condition. Their default count for the five-amino-acid whey arm conflicts with a Methods exclusion for antibiotics. The published means were retained without assigning a corrected count. The five-amino-acid forecast is unavailable for soy because the original soy isoleucine entry is missing. The choice of study followed qualitative outcome exposure. Numeric forecasts and the analysis plan were sealed before the implementing model requested the tabulated outcomes.

## Testing addition on a common glucose background

Opus's initial intake could not obtain the full Nilsson 2007 paper. We subsequently located the original article and executed the initial proposal. Twelve volunteers received 25 g glucose alone, or with whey or amino-acid additions. The five-amino-acid drink contained the same quantities as the two smaller blends combined. The final paper reports individual positive incremental areas over **90 minutes**, despite sampling through 120. Its three-amino-acid effect is +40% relative to glucose, differing from the earlier poster's +60%. [Nilsson et al., Tables 1–4](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/85.4.996) · [Earlier author poster](https://www.lth.se/fileadmin/nutrition/Postrar/Poster_Mikael_2006.pdf)

Measured glucose plus three amino acids gave 14.8 nmol·min/L. Glucose plus the other two gave 9.4. Glucose alone gave 10.6. Counting the shared glucose once predicts **14.8 + 9.4 − 10.6 = 13.6**, against **13.9** for the full mixture. Absolute error fell from 0.9 for the better single blend to 0.3 for the combination. These measurements came from the evaluation cohort. Clipping each curve below baseline makes addition of positive areas an empirical rule even when concentration trajectories could add. No paired uncertainty for the four-arm residual was reported. The earlier intact-whey/glucose panel had appreciably larger residuals on its own endpoint. The small residual here gives no basis for a fixed bonus for consuming ingredients together. [Mixture and food-only comparisons](nilsson/REPORT.md) · [Earlier component test](https://github.com/parthganguly/insight-insulin-app/blob/a6390ffa6affd9a24a180402642e858aa2e4b585/reports/science/2026-11-02-protein-protocols/README.md)

For the five-amino-acid drink itself, macro-derived energy predicts a mixture/glucose ratio of 1.296. The measured-component calculation gives 1.283 and the observed ratio is 1.311. Thus the inexpensive estimate was closer on that drink, even though the measured sum beat both constituent arms. Converting the energy ratio to native units would require a glucose-area calibration. This same-arm comparison is post hoc. Across all four drinks, macro-derived energy, total grams and a conditional leucine proxy gave four-arm errors of 0.122, 0.164 and 0.126. The proxy assumes compatibility between grams of protein served and the table's amino acids per gram of whey. The free blends themselves have known quantities. Two blends containing exactly the same leucine had printed means of 14.8 and 13.9 and shared a significance letter. Equivalence and a difference in their population means both remain unestablished.

## Scientific decision

Keep the component benchmark for studies that actually measured each drink on the same background. Retain total grams and a simple empirical level as food-input comparators. The amino-acid contrasts justify examining composition within a documented preparation, while the held-author result argues against implementing a generic adjustment from a protein label. The next useful comparison needs a controlled change in protein composition or dose within a common vehicle, with the same-protocol 120-minute native areas and paired contrasts. A particularly discriminating design changes leucine while matching total protein, form and energy, so the label and gram comparators predict a tie. Decision D and the consumer prediction target remain open.
